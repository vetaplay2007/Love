Four stories about whom we love, how we show it, and what we learn from one another.

A documentary longform about romantic relationships, self-acceptance, the bond between a person and an animal, and the friendships that make us feel at home.

I LOVE…

Finishing this sentence turned out to be less simple than it first appeared. When people were asked what they love, their answers brought together people, animals, universities, music, food, hobbies, memories, and the smallest moments of an ordinary day.

For one respondent, love fitted into a single word: “Living.” Someone mentioned kittens; someone else named manti, cold Coca-Cola, strawberries, and peonies.

I LOVE…

An informal survey with more than 100 respondents. These 74 selected answers reflect individual expressions of affection and personal preference; they are not intended to represent a statistically representative sample.

We are used to thinking of love as a story between two people. But these simple answers suggest that love is much more closely connected to everyday life. I spoke with four people. Their stories do not create one universal formula; each offers a different perspective on what it means to care, to feel close, and to love.

Story one · Romantic love

The quiet work of choosing each other

Romantic films hurry through the important moments. In Romeo and Juliet, love arrives almost instantly. In Pride and Prejudice the process is slower: first impressions are questioned and mistakes recognised. Darina and Adil's story has more in common with Austen's.

Nearly two years passed before they became a couple. Darina was nineteen and Adil twenty-one at the time of the interview. In ninth grade Adil once messaged her, and she did not even realise who he was. The turning point came at his graduation ceremony: that evening he replied to one of her social media stories. Soon they were walking almost every day.

On January 9, 2024, Adil came to her home for her birthday. He was the only guest. Her parents asked him to say a few words; he wished her success and promised to support her in whatever she wanted to achieve. Darina cried.

The first year brought misunderstandings and arguments. After one difficult conflict she travelled to Almaty and they took a short break, yet kept communicating and worrying about each other. They also wish each other good morning and good night; in more than three years, she says, only a few days have passed without those messages.

What she values most is his calmness. She is an anxious person; he tries to think rationally, listens, and sometimes simply says things will be all right. When he told her he was uncomfortable with the way she spoke to him, they talked, and she decided not to cross that boundary again.

“I choose him every time. And he chooses me every day.”— Darina

Story two · Self-love

Learning to make room for yourself

In Greta Gerwig's Barbie, the main character begins to question who she is beyond the familiar image. Behind the humour is a serious question: can we accept ourselves when we do not fit an image of perfection?

Aruzhan was eighteen and a second-year university student. For her, self-love is not the belief that she is better than others, nor an attempt to convince herself she is perfect. It is an understanding of her own value, even when something does not go according to plan.

She can be demanding of herself, especially in her studies. Over time she has learned to separate an assessment of an action from an assessment of herself: she can be unhappy with a mistake without deciding she is a bad person.

She speaks about boundaries too. Caring about others matters, but it should not become a habit of putting your own needs aside. The right to say “no” does not make a person selfish. And she tries to remember that a social media post shows only a small part of someone's life.

“I love myself simply because I am me.”— Aruzhan

Story three · An animal

When caring means letting go

In Troepolsky's White Bim Black Ear, the bond between a person and a dog is built on trust, dependence and responsibility. But an animal is also a living being with needs, fears and its own experience of the world.

Two years before the interview, the protagonist was walking home in cold, rainy weather and noticed a cat on the street, alone. Leaving it outside felt wrong, so they took it home. For the first couple of days it stayed on the balcony, until the parents found out. The cat was named Rory; the name simply came to mind.

But a younger brother, around four at the time, did not always know how to handle her gently. Rory grew frightened and began to hide from the people in the house. The family decided to find her another home, where she might feel more comfortable.

This story has no familiar happy ending. But giving Rory to another family does not mean there was no love. Strong attachment does not automatically mean an animal is doing well; care means noticing its fears, and changing our behaviour when its needs are not met.

“Love should not be reduced to the desire to keep it beside us at any cost.”

Story four · Friendship

A place where you can be yourself

Real friendships rarely begin with a dramatic promise. More often you notice there are people around whom you do not have to choose your words carefully.

For nineteen-year-old Magzhan, that group is Go Roflit. They met about a year before the interview, at a performance at ITO Music Club. What he values most is freedom: he can talk about almost anything without fear of being judged, and they try to understand one another before reacting with anger.

After a relationship ended, he kept everything to himself for a long time, until one day he broke down and cried in front of one of them. His friend did not try to fix anything. He hugged him and listened.

Sometimes support is a long conversation. Sometimes it is a hug after a journey to a cancelled rehearsal. And sometimes it is simply being able to come to a group of people without pretending everything is fine.

“Well, because I love them. Do I need any other reason?”— Magzhan

Chapter · Biology

What biology can explain — and what it cannot

Love is an emotion. It is also a process involving the brain, body, motivation, memory and social connection. There is no single “love chemical.”

129adults

Higher oxytocin concentrations were associated with more positive perceptions of romantic partners.

Shows an association; does not show that oxytocin causes love.
34young married couples

Higher oxytocin levels were associated with less relationship tension and higher reported relationship quality.

Cannot predict whether a relationship will last.

Both studies are discussed in Babková & Repiská (2025), The Molecular Basis of Love. No other figures are given here because none are cited.

What researchers know

Reward, motivation, social bonding and attachment involve several interacting biological systems.

What remains uncertain

Why one person bonds with another, and how far animal findings transfer to humans. Biology cannot explain why a birthday toast made Darina cry.

Chapter · Psychology

The psychology of connection

Attachment & emotional security

People differ in how they respond to closeness, distance and uncertainty. These are frameworks, not fixed labels or diagnoses. Story link: Darina values Adil’s calm and his willingness to listen when she is anxious.

Self-worth & self-compassion

Self-esteem, self-acceptance, self-compassion, boundaries and personal growth are different ideas; none requires being perfect. Story link: Aruzhan.

Friendship & loneliness

Dunbar (2025) links close relationships to wellbeing and notes friendships need time and effort. About five close friends is one layer in his research model — not a rule for everyone. Story link: Magzhan.

Human–animal attachment

Prato-Previde et al. (2022) examine empathy, attachment and anthropomorphism: bonds can benefit both sides, but an animal’s welfare can be overlooked. Story link: Rory.

Chapter · Digital age

How we talk about love today

What love looks like online

Declarations, couple photos, lists of green and red flags, love languages, other people’s polished moments. Useful vocabulary — restrictive when treated as universal rules.

What it can look like every day

A good-morning message. Support in a hard period. Talking after an argument. A hug without questions. Neither is more authentic than the other.

Chapter · Synthesis

Four stories, four forms of love

Everyone has their own way of finishing the sentence

Love does not have to look the same to be real. For one person, it begins with a confession. For another, it is the ability to say no. For someone else, it is taking a cold, frightened kitten off the street. And for someone, it is a friend's embrace when there is nothing useful to say.

There are so many ways to continue “I love…” And each one can be someone's real story.

Love does not have one shape.
It has many stories.
